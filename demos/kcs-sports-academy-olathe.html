The credentials

Four certifications. Here's what each one does for your athlete.

Youth speed training is an unlicensed field. Most of it is run by people who were fast once. These are the letters that mean someone sat an exam before writing a program for somebody else's kid.

National Sports Performance Association
C.S.A.C.
Certified Speed & Agility Coach
Sprint mechanics · acceleration · change of direction

The credential that separates a speed coach from a conditioning coach. NSPA's course is built around improving speed and agility for an athlete in any sport: how the foot strikes, how an athlete accelerates out of a cut, how to change direction without giving up a step. The goal is not more running. It is better running.

National Academy of Sports Medicine
C.P.T.
Certified Personal Trainer
The accredited foundation · programming · safe progression

NASM's CPT is the NCCA-accredited base credential and the most widely recognized certification in fitness. It is the one that says a coach was examined on anatomy, program design and how to load a body safely, before ever handing a young athlete a workout.

National Academy of Sports Medicine
C.E.S.
Corrective Exercise Specialist
Find the movement fault before it becomes an injury

This is the parent's credential. CES is movement assessment and correction: spotting the knee that collapses inward, the ankle that will not bend, the hip that never fully extends, then fixing the cause instead of stretching the symptom. Growing athletes compensate faster than they adapt, and a compensation trained at speed is how a season ends early.

National Academy of Sports Medicine
P.E.S.
Performance Enhancement Specialist
Strength and conditioning built for a sport, not a gym

NASM's strength-and-conditioning track: plyometrics, speed, agility, resistance training and Olympic lifting, organized across a season rather than dropped in at random. It is what turns a number in the weight room into a faster first step in a real game.

Verify any of them yourself — that is the point of a certification.

How training runs

Screen first. Then build. Then take it to the field.

The order matters. Loading speed onto a movement fault is how young athletes get hurt, so nothing gets programmed until the movement has been looked at.

STEP 01

Screen

A movement assessment before anything gets loaded. What bends, what does not, what compensates, and which side is doing more work than it should. This is where a training plan actually comes from.

Driven by NASM C.E.S.
STEP 02

Build

Correct the fault, then build strength and conditioning on top of clean movement. Progression is planned, not improvised, and it is scaled to a young athlete rather than borrowed from an adult program.

Driven by NASM C.P.T. + P.E.S.
STEP 03

Apply

Sprint mechanics, acceleration and change of direction, coached until they hold up under game speed. Speed that only shows up in a drill was never speed. It has to transfer to the field or the court.

Driven by NSPA C.S.A.C.

Why a screen comes first

Youth athletes grow in bursts, and a body that changed over a summer moves differently than it did in the spring. A screen catches what the growth spurt left behind before a season of sprinting bakes it in.

The coach

Ted Johnson

Movement Specialist · Owner, KC's Sports Academy

Ted runs KC's Sports Academy Youth Sports Performance in Olathe, coaching speed, movement quality and sport conditioning for young athletes across the Kansas City metro.

He is a certified speed coach through the National Sports Performance Association and holds three credentials from the National Academy of Sports Medicine: Certified Personal Trainer, Corrective Exercise Specialist and Performance Enhancement Specialist. That combination is deliberate. One credential covers how to make an athlete faster, one covers how to load a body safely, one covers how to find the movement fault first, and one covers how to build a season around all three.

He does not train athletes from a template. Sessions are built off what the screen turns up, then adjusted as the athlete grows and the season changes.
